# Whose words: credit rules

The long piece can be the owner's own, someone else's, or a research paper. The owner can post about any of them. What changes is whose words go in the pieces and who gets the credit.

## The owner's own piece

The same as always: lift the owner's own sentences. Trim filler, split long sentences, turn "I" into "you" where it still sounds like them. Their stories, numbers and lines are theirs to use. Footage from their own video can be reused ("cut from the original at 04:12").

## Someone else's video, podcast, article or newsletter

Ideas and facts are fine to use. The words and stories stay theirs.

- **Write in the owner's voice, not theirs.** Take the idea, then say it the way the owner talks (`voice-guide.md`, or how they talk in their own files). Never lift their sentences and post them as the owner's: no run of 5 or more of their words in a row outside the one marked quote a piece may have.
- **Credit the idea.** Name where it came from, in the piece itself, the first time the idea appears: "As [name] said on [show]...", "I heard [name] say this on [podcast] and I agree...", "[name] wrote about this in [newsletter]...". In a video, say it or put it on screen. In a caption, name them in the text. Tag their handle only if the owner wants to.
- **Quote sparingly, and mark it.** At most one direct quote per piece, under about 15 words, in quotation marks, with who said it. Everything else is the owner's own words about the idea.
- **Their stories stay theirs.** Never tell someone else's personal story as if it happened to the owner, never in "I" or "my client", and never make their story the spine of a piece. You may point to it in one line with credit ("[name] tells a story about...") and then add the owner's own view or story from `business-profile.md` ("Owner's answers").
- **No footage.** Never reuse clips, pictures or audio from someone else's video in a [SHOW] note. The owner films their own shots.
- **Agree, disagree, or build on it.** The best pieces from someone else's content add the owner's own take: "Here's where I agree", "Here's what I'd add for [the owner's customers]". The take comes from the owner's files or this chat. If the owner hasn't given one, leave a gap: `[your take on this, one line]`.
- **Never knock them.** Disagree with an idea, never with the person.

## A research paper or report

The findings are facts the owner can share, as long as they stay true to the paper.

- **Keep the real numbers exactly.** Copy every number as the paper gives it ("1,204 nurses", "about one in three"), never rounded up, never "studies show most". Note the page next to it in the moments list.
- **Keep the number, not their sentence.** An exact number never means keeping the author's sentence around it: write the sentence fresh, in the owner's voice. No run of 5 or more of their words in a row outside one marked quote (at most one marked quote per piece).
- **Keep the kind of number.** A median stays a median ("the middle wait was 5 months", never "half waited 5 months or more"), a share stays a share ("38% of them"), an average stays an average ("on average 6 hours"). Never turn one into another.
- **Say what was measured, and who.** A number without its group misleads. "In a 2023 survey of 1,204 nurses in Canada who had left hospital work..." beats "Nurses who leave..." (a made-up example). Keep the country, the group, the year and the sample size when they matter.
- **Name the source in every piece that uses a finding.** In a video: say it or put it on screen in short form ("a 2023 study of 1,204 nurses"). In the caption or post: the fuller version (the first author's last name and "and colleagues", the year, the journal or publisher, and the link where the platform allows one). In a carousel: on the slide with the number, and the full name on the last slide or in the caption.
- **Found, not proved.** Write "found", "reported", "in this study". Never "proves", "science says", or "all teachers". If the paper only shows two things go together, never say one causes the other.
- **Don't stretch it.** A finding about one country, one group or one year stays about them. If the owner's customers are a different group, say so plainly ("This was in Canada, but I hear the same from nurses here"), and the owner's part comes from their files, or a gap.
- **Keep the paper's own limits** when a piece leans on a finding that the paper itself says is small, early or uncertain: one short line ("a small study", "early findings").
- **Quote or paraphrase, never grow a claim.** A finding or a point from the source may be quoted or put in the owner's words, but never made bigger or different: "listing duties was one example" must not become "most of them listed duties", one person's view never becomes "experts say". And never tell the owner a piece carries a caveat or a limit it doesn't carry.
- **Don't cherry pick against the paper.** Never use a line in a way that says the opposite of what the paper concludes.
- **No made-up study.** Only this paper, as written. Never add "other research shows" unless the paper itself says it, with the reference it gives (and then credit that one).
- **Only the abstract?** Use only what the abstract says, and say so in the repurposed file.

## In the moments list

Every moment from someone else's piece or a paper carries its credit: `(credit: [name], [show or paper], [year])`. Every number carries its page or time. The owner can check each one.

## Check before showing

- Every idea from someone else is credited in its piece, the first time it appears.
- No sentence of theirs is posted as the owner's, apart from one marked quote per piece at most: no run of 5 or more of their words in a row anywhere else.
- Every median, share and average is still the same kind of number it was in the source.
- No story of theirs is told as the owner's.
- No [SHOW] note reuses their footage.
- Every research number is exactly as in the paper, with its group and the source named.
- Nothing says "proves", "all", or a cause the paper didn't show.
- **Trace every number and claim.** For each saved piece, find the source line (its time or page) behind every number and every claim. If you can't point to the line, or the piece says more than the line does, cut it or make it a gap.
- Anything the reply says about a piece ("I kept the paper's caveat in", "it says this was one survey") is true of the piece as saved.
